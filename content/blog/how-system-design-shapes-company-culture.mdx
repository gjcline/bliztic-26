# How System Design Shapes Company Culture

*Published: Monday, Aug 11, 2025*

Every founder tries to impact company culture through the use of meetings, values, and leadership. They all have a part in it, but they're only half of the story. If you really need to know something about a company's culture, don't start with the mission statement. Start with the systems.

Culture has been characterized as "how things get done around here." That is, the equipment you work with, the procedures you design, and how information moves through your business all shape behavior in slight ways without needing to be said out loud. They create habits individuals do, without saying it.

When a company does not have a process for knowledge capture, the culture reverts to guesswork and gatekeeping. When the deadlines are hidden or priorities are not centralized, groups learn to operate in silos. When progress is in someone's mind instead of in a shared system, teamwork becomes personality-driven rather than process-driven.

These habits harden over time. They become "the way things are." And that's how systems sneakily become culture.

---

## The accidental culture that isn't visible

Few teams consciously create their internal structure. They cobble together what gets the job done in the moment. That's natural, of course, in the beginning. But over time those improvised fixes begin to influence how your team communicates, sets priorities, and resolves problems.

We've worked with teams that once operated at a high level but gradually slipped into a culture of chaos. It wasn't the result of poor leadership. The real issue was that their internal systems made coordination unnecessarily difficult. The fastest-moving person started making all the decisions by default. Slack turned into the single source of truth, and instead of building scalable solutions, problems were handled behind closed doors.

On paper, they seem to have great cultures. In practice, their systems led to burnout and bottlenecks.

---

## Designing culture through better systems

If you want a culture of ownership, your systems have to be transparent and accountable. If you want a culture of collaboration, your tools have to break silo friction. If you want a culture of growth, your internal processes have to support experimentation without requiring total reinvention.

As an example, one of the founders we were working with was frustrated that her team wasn't stepping up. After we mapped their processes, it was clear they had no visibility into what anyone else was doing. There was nowhere to volunteer to assist, suggest ideas, or look forward to picking up dropped balls. We put a simple shared project center in place and retooled weekly check-ins around it. Within one month, initiative wasn't something she had to push. It was something the team naturally did, because now the system supported it.

---

## Culture as the result of structure

If your culture isn't where you want it to be, ask yourself where your current systems are pulling it down. Are the tools you're using supporting the values you're talking about? Are the workflows you're employing encouraging the behaviors you're rewarding?

The greatest cultural change never occurs by speech or new hire. It occurs by establishing an environment where the desired actions are the easiest way to move forward.

And that starts with your systems.
